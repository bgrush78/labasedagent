## Is Now Actually a Good Time to Buy Your First Home in LA?

There's no perfect answer here, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something. What I tell first-time buyers is this: rates have stabilized after a few volatile years, which means the "wait for a better rate" strategy has mostly stopped paying off. Buyers who've been sitting on the sidelines are competing with fewer people right now than they will once rates drop meaningfully, since a rate drop brings a wave of buyers back into the market with them.

The honest framing is that you're not timing a market, you're choosing a home and a payment you can live with, in a place you actually want to be. If that home exists and the payment works today, waiting for a marginally better rate rarely outweighs another year of rent with nothing to show for it. [I wrote more on this here](/blog/should-i-wait-for-interest-rates-to-drop) if you want the full breakdown.

## How Much Do You Actually Need?

This is the number that stresses people out the most, and it's usually smaller than they assume once we break it down.

**Down payment.** Contrary to popular belief, you don't need 20% down. FHA loans go as low as 3.5% down, and conventional loans can go as low as 3% for qualified first-time buyers. On a $700K starter home, that's the difference between needing $140,000 down and needing $21,000-$24,500 down.

**Closing costs.** Budget 2%-4% of the purchase price for closing costs: loan origination, title, escrow, inspections, and prepaid items like insurance and property tax. On that same $700K home, that's roughly $14,000-$28,000, though some of this can be negotiated as a seller credit depending on market conditions.

**Reserves.** Most lenders want to see 2-6 months of mortgage payments in reserve after closing, meaning money you still have left over, not money you're spending to close. This isn't a hard rule for every loan type, but it's worth planning around.

**PMI, and when it goes away.** If you put down less than 20%, you'll likely pay private mortgage insurance until you hit 20% equity. It's not the dealbreaker people think it is. It's a monthly cost that gets you into a home years earlier than waiting to save a full 20% down payment would.

## Down Payment Assistance Programs Worth Knowing About

This is the part most first-time buyers never hear about until it's almost too late to use it. A few programs I regularly walk clients through:

**CalHFA MyHome Assistance Program.** A deferred-payment junior loan (no payments until you sell, refinance, or pay off the first mortgage) that can cover a meaningful chunk of your down payment or closing costs. Income limits and county limits apply, and they change, so I confirm current numbers with a lender before we build a plan around it.

**FHA loans.** Backed by the federal government, these allow credit scores as low as 580 to qualify for the 3.5% down payment tier, with more flexible debt-to-income requirements than conventional loans. The tradeoff is mortgage insurance premiums that don't go away as easily as conventional PMI.

**VA loans.** If you or your spouse served, this is usually the best loan product available, full stop. Zero down payment, no PMI, and competitive rates.

**Local and county-level programs.** LA County and the City of Los Angeles periodically run down payment assistance and first-time buyer programs, and eligibility and funding levels shift. I keep a running list of what's currently active and check it with every first-time buyer I work with, since these programs can change the entire math of what's affordable.

## Loan Types for First-Time Buyers

**Conventional.** Best rates and terms if your credit and down payment are solid. Down payments as low as 3% for qualified first-time buyers, PMI drops off once you hit 20% equity.

**FHA.** More forgiving on credit score and DTI, 3.5% minimum down. Good option if your credit isn't quite where a conventional loan wants it, though mortgage insurance sticks around longer.

**VA.** Zero down, no PMI, strong rates, available to eligible service members and veterans. If you qualify, this is usually the easiest decision on this list.

**CalHFA.** State-run programs, often paired with the MyHome assistance program above, aimed specifically at making that first purchase more accessible for California residents.

None of these are universally "best." The right one depends on your credit, your available cash, and how long you plan to stay in the home. This is exactly the kind of comparison I run for every buyer before we start touring anything.

## Mistakes I See First-Time Buyers Make in LA

- **Getting pre-qualified instead of pre-approved.** Pre-qualification is a guess based on what you tell a lender. Pre-approval is underwritten. In a competitive market, sellers know the difference, and so should you before you fall for a home you can't actually get financed for.
- **Only shopping rate, not the full loan picture.** The lowest advertised rate isn't always the best product for your actual situation, especially once you factor in PMI structure, closing costs, and how long you plan to stay in the home.
- **Skipping the down payment assistance research.** These programs take time to set up. Waiting until you're in contract to ask about them is usually too late.
- **Underestimating the emotional pace of a competitive market.** LA moves fast on well-priced homes. Buyers who aren't mentally prepared to make quick, informed decisions often lose out on homes they actually liked.
- **Not budgeting for what happens after closing.** Moving costs, immediate repairs, furnishing, the first property tax bill. Build a cushion beyond just the down payment and closing costs.
